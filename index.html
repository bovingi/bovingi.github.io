Applied ecophysiology for a changing world

I am interested in the ways that climate change impacts forested ecosystems, with an emphasis on applications in land management. Using methods and insights from plant physiology, population ecology, fire ecology, and remote sensing, my work aims to build better management tools, inform restoration practices, and further our fundamental understanding of forest dynamics.

Research

Functional traits

Functional traits form the basis of countless theories and models of how trees and forests function. And yet, within species trait variation continues to baffle us by showing patterns contrary to what we would expect from cross-species analyses or from our typical understanding of trait-environment relationships.

Leveraging long-term demographic data, physiological trait measurements, and fine scale-microclimate data, my work seeks to understand species drought tolerance strategies and the drivers of within-species trait variation. Then, I seek to apply these insights to better understand how to predict and manage for the impacts of climate change.

Remote sensing and fire risk

Scaling leaf-level processes to landscape-scale patterns is an important element of two key management tools: remote sensing and modeling.

I am interested in applying our understanding of drought stress at the tissue and organism levels to improve how we interpret remotely sensed signals. Similarly, I seek to answer questions related to tissue hydration and leaf-level flammability to better understand how drought stress can influence fire risk. These small-scale changes can improve mechanistic models and predictions of wildfire behavior.

Seedling physiological tolerance

Tree recruitment and growth following wildfire is essential for the continuity of forested landscapes as we know them. As fires become more severe (impacting not only existing adult trees, but also their ability to spread seeds), management interventions focused on planting are especially important. Knowing what to plant and where to plant it (and where not to plant at all) is valuable insight for managers who must operate with limited resources, and this project aims to help clarify these unknowns by identifying bottlenecks in tree recruitment for Sierra Nevada conifer species impacted by recent mixed-severity burns.

I am part of a collaborative team investigating the relationships between fire severity and seedling growth and survival for a variety of conifers in the Sierra Nevada. We aim to understand how fire severity alters the microclimates that baby trees must inhabit on their journey from seed to sapling, as well as species' physiological thresholds for heat and drought tolerance. Additionally, we ask how shrubs might alter microclimates to impact seedling growth and survival.
